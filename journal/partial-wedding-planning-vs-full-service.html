Partial planning or full service? How to tell which you need

September 26, 20267 min readBy Madison Hasleton

Partial wedding planning is the right fit when you know what you want and have made some of the big decisions, but don’t want to carry the rest alone. You keep the vision and final say. Your planner fills in what’s missing—usually some vendors, the contracts and the design—and then runs the wedding day.

It’s the tier most couples end up in, and the hardest to size, because it sits between two clearer options. Here’s how it compares, and five situations to help you tell which one is yours.

The three tiers, side by side

  • Month-of coordination: you’ve planned the wedding. A coordinator takes over about six weeks out and runs the day. More on how that works.
  • Partial planning: you’ve started planning. A planner helps with the parts you haven’t done yet, then takes over six weeks out and runs the day.
  • Full service: a planner is with you from the beginning and handles essentially everything. What that includes.
The simplest way to tell them apart: how much is left to do, and how much of it do you want to do yourself?

What partial planning usually includes

Partial planning varies more from planner to planner than the other two tiers, so always ask what’s in it. Mine includes:

  • Vendor sourcing, negotiation and booking for up to five categories. You pick which five.
  • Contract review for every vendor you sign, including the ones you found yourself.
  • A design board, with a floor plan and rental list.
  • Monthly planning calls, plus email whenever you need it.
  • A budget, master timeline, vendor shortlists and templates for guests and seating.
  • The month-of takeover: about six weeks out, I become every vendor’s contact.
  • Rehearsal direction, and me plus an assistant for up to 10 hours on the day.

Compared with full service, it doesn’t usually include unlimited vendor sourcing, full styling and install oversight, guest logistics like hotel blocks and transport, or the rehearsal dinner and brunch.

Five scenarios

1. “We’ve booked the venue and photographer. That’s it.”

Partial planning. This is the classic case. You’ve made the two decisions that matter most for your date. What’s left is a lot of vendors, but you still want to choose them yourself. Partial planning gets you shortlists, contract review and a design without handing over control.

2. “We’ve booked almost everything. We just don’t want to run the day.”

Month-of coordination. If your vendors are booked and your design is settled, partial planning would mostly pay for help you no longer need. Coordination is built for this.

3. “We live in another state and we’re getting married in Utah.”

It depends on how much you’ve booked. Distance alone doesn’t mean full service. If you’ve booked the venue and several key vendors, partial planning works well from afar: I go to walkthroughs and tastings and send back notes. If you haven’t started, full service usually makes more sense, because the venue search and most vendor meetings need someone on the ground.

4. “We have strong opinions about the design, but no idea how to make it happen.”

Probably partial. You bring the vision. A design board, floor plan and rental list turn it into something vendors can quote. If you want someone to source every piece and oversee the install on the day, that’s full service.

5. “We both work long hours and planning is already stressing us out.”

Full service. Partial planning still asks you to make decisions every month and do some legwork. If you don’t have that time, or don’t want to spend your engagement that way, full service is the honest answer, even though it costs more.

Signs partial planning is the right fit

  • You enjoy parts of the planning and want to keep them.
  • You’ve booked your venue and a few key vendors, and the rest feels like a lot.
  • You want a professional to check the contracts and timeline before it’s too late to change them.
  • You want a real planner on the day, not a friend with a clipboard.
  • You’re 6 to 12 months out, which is when partial planning works best.

Common mistakes

Booking coordination when you still need several vendors. Coordination starts at six weeks out. If you still have vendors to find then, nobody is helping you find them.

Booking partial planning too late. Partial works best 6 to 12 months out. At three months, most of the vendor sourcing that makes partial worth it has already happened without help.

Not asking what “partial” means. Some planners’ partial planning is a few consultations. Others include vendor booking, design and a full wedding-day team. Ask how many vendor categories are included, and who’s there on the day.

The short version

Partial wedding planning suits couples who have started planning, want to keep the final say, and need help with the vendors, contracts and design still ahead, plus a planner to run the day. If you’ve already booked almost everything, month-of coordination is enough. If you’d rather hand over the whole engagement, choose full service.
