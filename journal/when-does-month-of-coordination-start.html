When does month-of wedding coordination actually start?

September 26, 20267 min readBy Madison Hasleton

Month-of wedding coordination starts about six weeks before the wedding. That’s the short answer, and it surprises people, because the service is usually sold as “day-of” and most of what you see happens on one Saturday. But the day only runs well because of the six weeks before it. This is what happens in that window, what you need to have ready, and when to book a coordinator for a Utah wedding.

Why six weeks?

Six weeks is about the minimum time it takes to learn someone else’s wedding well enough to run it.

By six weeks out, most of your decisions are made. Your vendors are booked, your invitations are out, and your design is mostly settled. That makes it the right moment to hand over. Any earlier and the plan is still changing under us. Any later and there isn’t enough time to confirm every vendor, catch the gaps and build a timeline everyone has actually read.

It’s also when vendor questions start arriving in bulk: final headcounts, load-in times, floor plans, and the florist asking where the arch goes. Month-of coordination means those questions stop landing in your inbox and start landing in mine.

What happens in the six weeks

Around six weeks out: the first handoff meeting. You walk me through the wedding, and I ask a lot of questions. Who’s coming, who’s booked, what’s in each contract, what the venue allows, which family members need a gentle word, and what you’re worried about. I leave with everything I need to take over.

Weeks five and four: I take over the vendors. I introduce myself to every vendor as your coordinator and confirm arrival times, what each one needs from the venue, and when final numbers are due. From here, vendor questions come to me.

Weeks four to three: the timeline takes shape. I build the minute-by-minute timeline around your venue’s hard times, your vendors’ needs and the light. In Utah, that last part matters: a late-June sunset in Salt Lake is around 9 p.m., while a December sunset is closer to 5. Portraits and the ceremony get placed first, and everything else flexes around them.

The second handoff meeting. We go through the draft timeline together, settle the last open questions and make sure nothing important lives only in your head.

The final two weeks. The finished timeline goes to every vendor, your wedding party and your family. I chase the last confirmations and build the day-of plan, from who holds the rings to who takes the gifts home.

The week of. I walk the venue with your venue coordinator and we trade phone numbers. Then comes the rehearsal, where everyone learns where to stand and when to walk.

The day. I run it with an assistant, so nobody needs you for anything but getting married.

What you should have done before the handoff

Month-of coordination is built for couples who have done the planning. At the first handoff meeting, it helps to have:

  • Your vendors booked. Venue, catering, photographer, florist, music, officiant, and anything else you’re bringing in.
  • Your contracts. I read them for arrival windows, overtime rates and anything the venue requires.
  • A rough guest count and a sense of when you’ll have final RSVPs.
  • Your design decisions. Palette, tablescape, ceremony setup, and any rentals you’ve ordered.
  • The non-negotiables. The song, the grandparent who needs a chair near the aisle, the photo you absolutely want.
  • The worries. The things keeping you up at night are the things I most want to hear about.

It doesn’t need to be tidy. A folder, a spreadsheet, a stack of emails: I’ll sort it.

How late can you book month-of coordination?

For a Utah wedding, I’d book at least four months out. Popular dates here—Saturdays from May through October—fill up, and coordinators book up along with them. Booking early also means you can ask questions before the handoff, rather than discovering at six weeks out that something should have been decided at ten.

If your wedding is sooner than that, ask anyway. The six-week handoff can be compressed when it has to be.

Is month-of coordination the same as wedding management?

Mostly, yes. “Wedding management,” “month-of coordination,” “day-of coordination” and “wedding-day coordination” are different names for the same job: someone takes over a plan you’ve made and carries it out. The name matters much less than the scope. When you’re comparing coordinators, ask:

  1. When do you start working on my wedding?
  2. How many meetings do we have before the day?
  3. Will you contact my vendors directly, or do I?
  4. Who writes the timeline, and who gets a copy?
  5. Is the rehearsal included?
  6. How many hours are you on site, and who is with you?

If the answers are “the morning of,” “none,” “you,” “you” and “no,” that’s a day-of attendant, not a coordinator. That can be fine for a very small wedding, but know what you’re buying.

When month-of isn’t enough

Month-of coordination assumes the planning is done. If you’re six months out with the venue booked and not much else, you’ll probably want more help, because nobody is sourcing your vendors or checking contracts before you sign. That’s the gap partial planning fills: help with the vendors and design still ahead of you, plus the same month-of handoff and day-of team at the end.

The short version

  • Month-of coordination starts about six weeks before the wedding, with a handoff meeting.
  • Your coordinator takes over every vendor, builds and shares the timeline, runs the rehearsal and runs the day.
  • Book it at least four months out for a Utah wedding, and earlier for a May–October Saturday.
  • Have your vendors, contracts and decisions ready for the handoff.
